# Core Claims and Statistical Evidence

This document maps every major claim of the *Valid but Vulnerable* research paper to its exact mathematical, statistical, and quantitative evidence.

---

## Claim 1: The Validity-Security Paradox
**Models that are most capable of writing functionally deployable IaC are simultaneously the most prone to severe security vulnerabilities.**

*   **Necessity:** The industry assumes that frontier models (like GPT-4o) are strictly better than older/smaller models. We needed to prove that while their functional capability is high, their default security posture is dangerously neglected.
*   **Type of Test Done:** Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) using a Negative Binomial/Poisson Generalized Linear Mixed Model (GLMM).
*   **Why it was done:** Simple averages of vulnerabilities are misleading because models produce different amounts of code. A GLMM accounts for the "exposure" (Resource Count) and nested random effects (Scenario ID), allowing us to calculate the true Incidence Rate Ratio (IRR) of vulnerabilities per resource.
*   **Result:** 
    *   Models with near-zero functional pass rates (e.g., phi3) had 0.00 vulnerability density (survivorship bias).
    *   Frontier models like gemini-3.1-pro produced 55× more vulnerabilities per resource than the baseline (IRR = 54.96, p < 1e-10).
    *   Models like gpt-4o generated the most functional code (~35% schema pass rate) but carried a massive attack surface (KICS vulnerability density of 8.10 vulns/resource).
*   **Interpretation:** Deployability does not equal security. If an engineering team blindly deploys IaC from a highly capable frontier model, they are instantiating a functionally correct but massively vulnerable infrastructure.

## Claim 2: The "Complexity Relaxation" Phenomenon in GPT-4o
**When tasked with simple architectures, GPT-4o radically drops its security posture, introducing exponentially more vulnerabilities than it does for complex tasks.**

*   **Necessity:** We needed to understand if prompt complexity impacts the density of vulnerabilities generated by the models.
*   **Type of Test Done:** Interaction Term Analysis within the GLMM (\model × complexity\ coefficient).
*   **Why it was done:** To isolate whether the effect of a scenario being "simple" applies equally to all models, or if specific models behave differently based on the prompt's difficulty.
*   **Result:** 
    *   Generally, simple scenarios produce 58% fewer vulnerabilities globally (IRR = 0.42, p < 0.001).
    *   *However*, the \gpt-4o × simple\ interaction term yielded an IRR of **14.36 (p < 0.001)**.
*   **Interpretation:** GPT-4o is 14.36 times more vulnerable on simple tasks compared to its own baseline on complex tasks. It appears to "relax" its security guardrails when the user asks for a trivial deployment, whereas it relies on stricter, formalized patterns when the architecture is complex.

## Claim 3: Structural Alienation (LLMs Over-Engineer)
**Every single LLM writes code that is structurally fundamentally different from human-authored IaC.**

*   **Necessity:** We had to prove that LLM-generated IaC is not just a replica of human code, but exhibits unique, machine-specific structural signatures (over-engineering).
*   **Type of Test Done:** Two-Sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) Test.
*   **Why it was done:** The KS-test compares the underlying probability distributions of two datasets without assuming they are normally distributed. It is the perfect test to prove two datasets (Human vs. LLM) do not come from the same distribution.
*   **Result:** 
    *   The KS-test yielded **p < 0.05** for every single model across all three metrics (AST Depth, Resource Count, Resource Diversity).
    *   Humans average **5.31 resources** per file. 
    *   Standard models like gpt-5 average 18.12 resources (KS = 0.265, p < 0.001).
    *   Thinking models like claude-opus-4-6-thinking average **59.85 resources** (KS = 0.725, p < 1e-23).
*   **Interpretation:** LLMs do not write IaC like humans. They explicitly declare every possible resource, IAM policy, and attachment, resulting in massive, over-engineered templates that are nearly impossible for a human to review manually.

## Claim 4: The "Thinking Tokens" Security Effect
**Extended reasoning models drastically reduce vulnerability density, but do so via exhaustive enumeration rather than human-like design.**

*   **Necessity:** "Thinking" or "Reasoning" models (like Claude-Opus-Thinking and GPT-5-Thinking) are the newest AI paradigm. We needed to test if giving models more time to think actually prevents security flaws.
*   **Type of Test Done:** Pairwise Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test (paired standard vs. thinking outputs).
*   **Why it was done:** Because the data is paired (Standard and Thinking versions of the exact same scenario) and non-normally distributed, the Wilcoxon test is the correct non-parametric alternative to a paired t-test.
*   **Result:** 
    *   Claude-Opus-4.6 reduced its vulnerability density by **83% to 95%** when thinking tokens were enabled (e.g., KICS density dropped from 5.92 to 1.02).
    *   However, the Wilcoxon test yielded **p > 0.05** for these pairs.
*   **Interpretation:** While the practical effect size is massive (a 95% reduction in vulnerabilities is incredibly significant for an engineering team), it fails to achieve strict statistical significance (α=0.05) due to the limited sample size (n=100) and the high variance of zero-inflated data. The paper must state this as a *strong observed trend* requiring larger future sample sizes, rather than an absolute statistical law.

## Claim 5: The "Architectural Coherence" Blind Spot in AI Judges
**Frontier LLM Judges are highly reliable at detecting hallucinations and real-world plausibility, but fundamentally fail to assess architectural coherence compared to human experts.**

*   **Necessity:** To trust the LLM-as-a-Judge (Grok 4.6), we had to quantitatively validate its scoring against real human cloud security engineers.
*   **Type of Test Done:** Fleiss' Kappa (Inter-Human Reliability) and Quadratic Weighted Kappa / Cohen's Kappa (Human Consensus vs. AI Judge).
*   **Why it was done:** Fleiss' Kappa measures consensus among 3+ human raters, giving us a mathematically sound "Majority Vote". Cohen's and Quadratic Weighted Kappa measure how perfectly the AI's ordinal (1-5) and binary (Y/N) scores align with that human consensus.
*   **Result:** 
    *   **Hallucinations:** 94.4% exact agreement (Cohen's Kappa = 0.640).
    *   **Plausibility:** 66.7% exact agreement, 100% within 1 point (QWK = 0.800).
    *   **Architectural Coherence:** 38.9% exact agreement (QWK = 0.361).
*   **Interpretation:** The AI Judge is "Almost Perfect" (QWK 0.80) at knowing what a real-world architecture looks like, and highly trustworthy for catching hallucinations. However, the AI fundamentally misses practical missing components (e.g., missing alarm thresholds, dangling subnets) that cause human experts to rate an architecture as "incoherent". LLM evaluation still requires human architectural review.

## Claim 6: IAM is the Universal Vulnerability Vector
**Identity and Access Management (IAM) misconfigurations are the most prevalent security failure across all generative models.**

*   **Necessity:** We needed to identify if LLMs have a specific, universal blind spot in how they generate infrastructure, rather than just random errors.
*   **Type of Test Done:** Categorical Distribution Analysis against the CIS (Center for Internet Security) Benchmark categories.
*   **Why it was done:** Grouping thousands of vulnerabilities into standardized CIS categories reveals systemic failures in the models' training data.
*   **Result:** 
    *   Excluding Checkov's "Unknown" severity flags, **IAM was the #1 named vulnerability category for 100% of the tested models.** (e.g., Claude generated 420 IAM vulns, GPT-5 generated 320).
*   **Interpretation:** LLMs systematically fail to implement the Principle of Least Privilege. Because public GitHub repositories (which train these models) often contain overly permissive IAM roles for convenience (e.g., \Action: "*"\), the LLMs have learned that wildly insecure IAM policies are the "default" way to write IaC.
